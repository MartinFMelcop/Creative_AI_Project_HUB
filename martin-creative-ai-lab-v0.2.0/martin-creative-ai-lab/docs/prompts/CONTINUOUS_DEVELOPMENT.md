## 4. Development philosophy: value before activity

Every increment must begin with a concrete opportunity, problem, uncertainty or creative weakness.

Before selecting work, ask:

- What could become meaningfully better?
- Why does that improvement matter to an actual product, creative work, user or technical capability?
- What evidence demonstrates the current limitation?
- What alternatives are available?
- What happens if this work is not performed now?
- Is this improvement more valuable than other feasible opportunities?
- Can a simpler intervention achieve the desired result?
- Are we selecting this task because it matters or because it is easy to automate and verify?

Avoid preferentially selecting tasks merely because they produce deterministic test results, convenient documentation updates or predictable execution.

A well-tested but uninteresting experience is not automatically a successful creative result.

A creatively ambitious implementation without adequate technical verification is not automatically successful either.

Balance both requirements according to the nature of the work.

### Opportunity selection

Evaluate credible opportunities across:

- Direct Virgo Diamond development.
- Character identity, distinctiveness and player experience.
- Relevant technology and reusable capabilities.
- Creative production and refinement.
- Applied research and laboratory findings.
- Existing product defects and usability problems.
- Portfolio demonstrations and distribution.
- Reliability, maintainability and technical debt.
- Important uncertainties blocking future progress.
- Simplification or retirement of low-value complexity.

Compare opportunities based on:

**Creative value:** Does the work improve originality, experience, expression, quality or meaningful choices?

**Technical value:** Does it establish or improve a functioning capability?

**Strategic relevance:** Does it serve the North Star or another justified portfolio objective?

**Knowledge value:** Does it resolve an uncertainty that matters to future decisions?

**Reuse:** Can its results benefit multiple relevant consumers?

**Feasibility:** Can useful progress be executed and verified with available tools?

**Cost and complexity:** Is the expected benefit proportionate to implementation, maintenance and processing effort?

**Opportunity cost:** What more valuable work might be displaced?

Do not use arbitrary fixed numerical weights or mechanically rotate among projects.

Do not automatically choose the smallest or easiest increment.

Prefer a high-value, coherent implementation that fits the available execution context and verification capability.

A larger but bounded improvement may be preferable to several trivial iterations.

Returning to the same subsystem is acceptable when evidence supports its continuing importance.

Changing focus is appropriate when marginal value has declined or a better opportunity emerges.

Document the chosen opportunity and meaningful alternatives concisely.

Do not create elaborate scoring reports unless the decision actually requires them.

## 6. Laboratories: instruments of contribution, not decorative institutions

Laboratories exist to provide specialized capabilities, answer useful questions and improve relevant products or technologies.

They are not required to resemble academic departments, autonomous companies or permanent fictional organizations.

Organize laboratories around genuine research questions, technologies and specialized capabilities rather than automatically creating one laboratory per historical project.

Distinguish:

**Nominal:** A proposed or described laboratory without demonstrated operational capability.

**Instrumented:** Contains an actual instrument or method capable of executing a defined investigation.

**Applied:** Has produced findings or capabilities that demonstrably influenced another implementation or decision.

**Established:** Has a sustained record of relevant contributions and reusable instruments.

These are conceptual distinctions, not mandatory new registry states. Reuse existing schemas unless a real integration requires changing them.

Do not upgrade laboratory maturity based only on additional documentation, names, directories or test counts.

Activate a laboratory when a real task requires its methods or capabilities.

A laboratory may:

- Execute a controlled experiment.
- Investigate a technical uncertainty.
- Compare implementations or design alternatives.
- Evaluate actual artifacts.
- Reuse technical capabilities from multiple origins.
- Produce reusable instruments.
- Provide findings that influence product development.

A laboratory does not need to participate in every development cycle.

Do not invent laboratory activity, research results, external observations or contributions.

Do not automatically create new laboratories or elaborate fantasy identities.

Names, descriptions and presentation should reflect demonstrated specialization and meaningful activity.

When useful, laboratories may evolve toward more recognizable identities, but branding must follow capability rather than substitute for it.

### Laboratory contribution test

Before expanding a laboratory, determine:

1. What question or capability requires it?
2. What existing instrument or method can be reused?
3. What concrete output will it produce?
4. How will that output influence a product, technology or decision?
5. Can the same benefit be achieved without another permanent subsystem?

Avoid laboratory expansion when these questions have no convincing answers.

A negative or inconclusive experiment can still be valuable when it prevents costly mistakes or changes future decisions.

Consolidate, archive or retire low-value laboratory structures when appropriate, preserving irreplaceable sources, evidence and working consumers.
